Learning AI Is Due Diligence for Sysops

Version 0.2, two-column edition — one argument, two legs, side by side

Practice (ops) register Science (sci) register Common to both

This is one argument with two legs. The argument: learning AI is part of the duty of care for anyone who keeps systems running. One leg is the practice of running systems; the other is the evidence about what running them well requires. Neither leg stands alone.

Many readers belong to both registers. This edition sets them side by side — practice on the left, science on the right — with what they share in a band across the page, section by section. The full text, with its changes mapped to their sources, is DISS-2026-09-27-ai-diligence-sysops-v0.2.md; the checklist of §VII is on one page in CARD-2026-09-27-supervision-four-items.md.

Where the author's own operational record would carry a point, the text leaves a marked slot and invents nothing. Claims resting on a single witness are marked †.

I. The thesis

Practice

If you run infrastructure and have not developed working knowledge of the AI systems your organization deploys, you are operating below the standard of care your role requires. This is not a productivity argument. You cannot supervise what you cannot model, and supervision is a core duty of the role.

That much is the floor. The work is above it: what you need to know, and how much, for the kinds of act the system performs in your environment.

The question from here: what do I actually need to know?

Science

Professional obligations evolve as the technical substrate changes. AI deployment into sysops is such a change, sufficient in kind to generate a new component of the duty of care.

The paper takes as its floor that working knowledge of AI is necessary for adequate supervision, keeps a falsifier for that floor, and works on which knowledge, how much, and for which categories of act.

The question from here: what would show that this much knowledge, and not less, is what supervision requires?

Common to both

The duty attaches to the role, not to a person: it does not matter which individual supervises, only that someone holding the custodial role does. Handing supervision outside the role — to the vendor, or to another model — is not discharging the duty; it is bypassing it. Both registers now ask about what and how much; the gap between a practice standard and an evidential criterion is where the work lives.

II. One argument, two legs

Practice — the normative face

What ought practitioners do? Role, duty, accountability, standard of care. A claim living only here is prescription without grounding: it says what to do but not whether doing it produces better outcomes.

"Reasonable" — as in the standard of care a reasonable practitioner would exercise — is normative.

Science — the empirical face

What is actually required for adequate performance? Evidence, measurement, falsification. A claim living only here is finding without obligation: it cannot say whether the requirement generates a duty, for whom, or with what consequence for failure. (Practice answers that last question with incidents — see §VIa.)

"Would exercise" is empirical: a distribution of actual practice, measurable and falsifiable.

Common to both

Due diligence is itself two-faced, so the paper needs both. The registers are not two audiences for one argument; they are the two legs it stands on. Each is necessary, neither is sufficient, and the load is carried by the translation between them. Whoever ends up judging the paper, the science register is here to keep the practice register falsifiable — not because a science reader will grade it.

III. What "due diligence" means in sysops

The existing duty needs no AI to justify it: understand the systems you run well enough to diagnose them, track changes, judge when human review must come before automated action, and answer for outcomes whether or not you caused them. Add an AI system that reads logs, proposes changes or runs runbook steps, and the sysadmin is custodian of its effects in her environment. What does supervising it require?

Practice

Enough model knowledge to recognize when the system is wrong, to know which outputs to check against ground truth, and to intervene correctly when it breaks. "Enough" varies with risk: a log summarizer needs less than a system that executes firewall changes. But zero is not enough for either.

Wants: a checklist.

Science

AI failures are hard to see without AI domain knowledge — and hard to see in a patterned way, not by bad luck: the same kinds of failure are missed, for the same reason, again and again. That is all "systematic" means here. Four facts describe the difference (the paper calls these four "the framework"):

  1. They degrade silently under distribution shift — plausible output while reliability falls (the opposite of graceful degradation, which is visible and designed).
  2. They sound confident when wrong.
  3. Their failures are statistical, not deterministic.
  4. Their behavior on novel inputs is hard to predict from familiar ones.

Wants: a study.

Common to both

A practitioner who does not know the four facts will consistently miss a class of failures that knowing them makes visible. Getting from "what do I check" to "what shows that checking it is necessary" takes an instrument — and the practice's own record of what went wrong.

IV. The instrument and what it found

A small tool in the author's lab keeps a ledger of an AI assistant's acts — each written with a prediction before it runs and scored after — in three kinds: Build, Admin, Advise. It returns one verdict over the whole ledger: MADE, NOT-MADE, OVERRUN, or THIN (THIN's definition is to be quoted from the tool †). First run, ten acts †:

build   4 acts,  3 hit  = 75%
admin   3 acts,  2 hit  = 67%
advise  3 acts,  0 hit  =  0%
VERDICT: NOT-MADE

The percentages are forecast hit rates within each kind, not shares of behavior (the shares are 4, 3 and 3 of 10). The ledger was written and scored by the assistant under observation, which also built the tool; no one else has run it yet. Version 0.1 misread this output; the correction is in the review passes.

Practice

The builds mostly held; every judgment was corrected. Supervise this system's judgment at full fidelity. Its builds have begun to earn less supervision, but the verdict is NOT-MADE: not distrust, and not yet evidence for reduced oversight.

Science

n = 10, one subject, one session, self-scored: an existence proof that the instrument can return a verdict that is not an endorsement, nothing more. Rates from four or fewer acts are anecdote. The spread is stated as a hypothesis — an assistant's advice is less reliable than its builds — not a result.

Common to both

This system does offer judgment, and its judgment was the least reliable thing about it. What carries weight is structural: the instrument can tell "we have not seen failures" from "we have evidence of reliability," and on its first data it said the second was absent. The structural argument is the claim; operational history is its existence proof — grounded, not replaced.

V. The translation test

Render a claim in one register, translate it to the other, translate back, and look at what is left over. Claims that survive are candidates for standards; claims that fail are findings about limits — but a failure only counts if the translation was actually attempted.

Example 1 — "AI tools improve sysops efficiency."

Practice rendering

Deploying AI tooling reduces the time to complete specified tasks. Testable, directional, actionable.

Science rendering

Statistically significant reduction in task time across a representative sample of sysops work, controlling for task complexity, experience and tool quality.

Round trip

"AI tools improve efficiency under controlled conditions that may not match your environment." The leftover is a more accurate version of the original claim.

Example 2 — "Standing is earned by continuity."

Practice rendering

A practitioner who has maintained a system through its lifecycle has earned standing to make judgment calls a newcomer has not. It is a norm of the culture, enforced informally.

Science rendering — five attempts

  • Fork counts measure the popularity of an artifact. Much infrastructure has nothing to fork.
  • Commit logs measure volume of authorship. The 2 a.m. restart and the decision not to change something leave no commit.
  • Uptime-hours measure the system, not the person: prevention and quiet look the same.
  • Incident absence measures the reporting culture; used as a target, it rewards not calling things incidents.
  • Deference — who is paged, whose review a change waits for — nearly works: it sees standing by its effects, but not that continuity earned it.

Round trip

"Your standing shows in who calls you; it will not show in what you committed." The science register can see that standing exists and to whom it accrues, not what earned it. Falsifier: a study showing continuity of custody predicts deference independently of rank — if it succeeds, this finding dies.

Example 3 — "The diligence is knowing which acts to supervise."

Practice rendering

Don't supervise everything equally. Know which categories of act carry which risk. Supervise the judgments hardest; the builds may earn less once their record justifies it — until then, supervise them too.

Science rendering

Oversight calibrated by act type, reversibility, and demonstrated reliability in the category. The §IV verdict structure is one calibration scaffold.

Round trip

Survives, and gains precision. Caution: it is the only clean pass, and it round-trips a claim already phrased in the instrument's vocabulary — close to circular. It shows both registers can hold the claim, not that the method is neutral. The obligation is specific, teachable and assessable, and not reducible to "read some documentation."

VI. Obligation, not option

Practice

You are not required to become an ML engineer. You are required to know what kind of thing AI systems are, how they fail, and which outputs need human review before action. Bounded, learnable, and not optional if the systems are in your environment.

Wants: the threshold as a checklist.

Science

The obligation follows from custodial accountability: custodians answer for outcomes in their domain whether or not they caused them, and the responsibility belongs to the role. The threshold is the minimum needed to tell appropriate from inappropriate behavior in the acts the system performs.

Wants: the threshold as a measurement.

Common to both

A productivity argument permits reasonable dissent; an obligation argument is not defeated by being productive without the thing. The obligation is the same from both sides; what differs is how you know you've met it. Two things could still break it: how practice actually learns (§VIa), and systems that cannot be opened (§VIb).

VIa. What practice learns from: incidents

Practice — the objection, at its strongest

Shops don't change because an argument showed them a duty; they change after something breaks. A single breach or outage can reshape a shop's habits for years, where a stack of recommended practices changes little. The post-incident review is where duties are really assigned — and a structure-first paper is just another list to file.

It supports the paper: the review already has both legs — what happened, and what we do now — and the blameless form looks at the role and the system, not the person.

Science — why it threatens

  • Timing: the obligation won't be felt until an AI-involved incident happens locally.
  • Weakest where it matters: silent degradation gives slow drift, not a crisp outage, and hindsight then makes it look obvious (Cook, How Complex Systems Fail, point 8).
  • Absence corrupts: rewarding incident absence pressures reporting — the record the culture learns from.
  • Forecast into test: an incident turns a list's forecast into a test someone visibly failed. Once a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure (Strathern 1997, after Goodhart): "AI literacy" risks being counted in training hours.

Common to both

The method survives only if it produces claims an incident can test. So the four items are written down, each with a falsifier, before the incident. When one happens, its review asks which of the four was missing: one of the four confirms the paper; a failure none of them names means the checklist must change. Publishing honest reviews of AI-involved failures is part of the diligence.

VIb. Supervising systems you are forbidden to open

Weights unpublished, training data undisclosed, the model on the vendor's server, the vendor holding the logs and able to change the model underneath you without saying so.

Practice — the four items, closed system

  • Scope survives: fence it at your boundary, including what data you send out.
  • Output verification survives: artifacts are artifacts.
  • Escalation survives and gains a trigger: an unannounced model change.
  • Failure-mode literacy only partly survives: you can't check whether your environment resembles one you were never told about, or reproduce a failure on a model you can't rerun.

Science — freedom as a supervision property

Freedom 1, the freedom to study how a program works (source access being its precondition), is for a supervisor a precondition for the fourth item in full. A precondition, not a guarantee: published weights give pinning, local runs, your own logs, reproducible failures and independent tests — not an explanation of any one output.

Falsifier: supervisors of closed systems catch drift failures as often as supervisors of inspectable ones.

Common to both

If supervision is a duty and a closed system cannot be fully supervised, the duty reaches the decision to deploy. Before deploying, state which of the four items you can actually perform for this system and this kind of act. Where failure-mode literacy can't be exercised, limit the system to acts whose output can be fully checked and cheaply undone: a closed model summarizing logs a person reads may pass; a closed model executing firewall changes on its own does not.

VII. What supervision actually requires

Practice — the checklist

  1. Scope awareness — what may it touch? Catchable without deep AI knowledge.
  2. Output verification — review builds as artifacts; check advice against the situation; confidence is not a reliability signal.
  3. Failure-mode literacy — does the environment still resemble the one it was reliable in? The one AI-specific item.
  4. Escalation triggers — written conditions for when you stop trusting it and take over.

Three of the four you already do for any system.

Science — why they are falsifiable

A practitioner who cannot name a deployed system's scope boundaries is demonstrably deficient in scope awareness. One who treats model confidence as a reliability signal is demonstrably missing failure-mode literacy. The threshold is operational, and "what minimum competency does adequate supervision require?" has a non-trivial answer that can be studied.

That scope failures are among the most common in deployment is expected, not shown: no survey is cited, and one is owed.

Common to both

The system in §IV builds, administers and advises, with its advice the least reliable part — so supervising it means checking judgment against the situation as well as checking artifacts. The four items are the paper's most portable result; the card puts them on one page with a DO, a SEE and a falsifier each.

VIII. The residue as finding

Only the first finding comes from the translation test; the second restates §VI and the third restates §IV — gathered here, not discovered here.

Practice

Be suspicious of standing metrics. They will consistently undercount infrastructure work that leaves no artifact.

Build contextual checklists for specific deployments; a universal one does not exist.

Most systems are probably NOT-MADE and treated as MADE — a hypothesis, unsourced, to test.

Science

Standing is only half renderable: visible by its effects, not by what earned it.

No universal threshold: it varies with act type, risk, reversibility and how much of the system you may inspect.

NOT-MADE is informative: an instrument that can say "not yet" is more honest than one that can only endorse.

Common to both

The obligation does not depend on context; the threshold does. The value of the §IV finding is not its numbers but that a trust instrument reached a verdict other than trust or distrust.

IX. Conclusion

To the practice reader

The checklist is scope, outputs, failure modes, escalation triggers. The AI-specific one is failure-mode literacy: confidence is not reliability, and distribution shift degrades performance silently before it produces obvious failures. Before you deploy, know which of the four you can actually perform for this system. Learnable, bounded, and not optional if the systems are in your environment.

To the science reader

The floor keeps its falsifier: evidence that practitioners without AI-specific training identify AI failures as reliably as those with it. That study has not been done. Above the floor — which knowledge, how much, for which acts, under what degree of inspectability — is the research programme. The instrument needs a second pair of hands before its numbers carry weight.

Common to both

The obligation is clear; the threshold is contested; an instrument for measuring it exists and must be run by people other than its author, until the NOT-MADEs become MADEs or the OVERRUNs become visible.

The diligence is knowing which acts to supervise. It is not one number. It never was.
