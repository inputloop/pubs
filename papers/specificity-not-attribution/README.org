#+TITLE: Specificity, not attribution
#+SUBTITLE: A blind rerun of claim C1 (DRAFT, unbranded)
#+DATE: 2026-09-30

* What this is

A short paper on one question: when you write rules for an AI assistant, does it help to present them as a named
author's own words? A kit made that claim (called C1). Its first test seemed to back it, but the named lines were
also more specific than the plain ones they were compared with.

This rerun split the two. Three sets of rules (named, the same rules with no name, and general ones) met the
same five tasks. Two AI graders scored the fifteen answers blind.

- The name added nothing: 0 of 5 tasks favoured it, 5 ties, under both graders.
- Being specific helped: better on 4 of 5 tasks for one grader and 3 of 5 for the other, on behaviour.
- Limits: both specific sets hit a perfect score (no room to show a gap); one AI wrote all the answers knowing
  the hypothesis; only 5 tasks; all models from one vendor family.

In practice: write rules concretely, in your project's own terms, and keep the author's name as a record of
where a rule came from (provenance), not as its voice.
