1 Door

A ghostwheel is a set of computers in rings, so that when one is away, the others carry on. The first design has seventeen of them. Fifteen stand in three rings of five, and a ring is available while any three of its five are. The same fifteen also make five rings of three. The edge ring holds all seventeen, in service or not: it is where hosts are added and taken away, and where the rings are built.

Nobody picked those numbers one by one. Take seventeen, keep two aside (one to build on, one to test on), and fifteen are left. Divide by three, and each big ring has five. Turn that over, and the small rings hold three.

What runs on the wheel is C4, published in 2025 as "Continuous Creation, Command & Control, a literate technology control framework". Its aim, in one line: make the thing findable later, by someone who was not there, without asking them to trust you. No AI is needed to use it.

Spoke and Pin are this book's two guides. Spoke sees. Pin writes down what Spoke saw, with when and where. Each small mark on this page opens what Pin wrote.

2 Letters

C4 keeps its words in rooms, and every room is one letter. There are seventeen. They live in a plain text file called m/t, one line for each letter. Each line gives its letter four names, under four columns that have names of their own: scalar, group, command and tag. A's line reads ASSET, ARRAY, ADD, ARCHIVE. S's line reads SCALAR, SPOKE, SELECT, SEMAPHORE, and that is where Spoke got a name.

That makes sixty-eight words. Sixty-four of them are common English; only four (asset, obfuscate, spoke and taxonomy) are claimed nowhere else. The busy words are on purpose: when two rooms both need the word "map", the letter keeps them apart.

Nine more letters hold values and force stops. Pin has two notes about them that differ by one word: one says Q is QUERY, the table says QUERY-STABLE. Both are kept, because the difference is worth knowing.

Saying one idea four ways is not a muddle. C4 asks for at least two ways to say everything, and asks that they balance. The facing pages of this book try to do the same.

3 Seams

One word can carry several meanings; C4 keeps them all. Corwin gave three of its words their meanings on 2026-08-03, and they explain each other.

A seam is "an opportunity to better pin someone's language": not a defect, not a gap to fill, but an opening, where someone gets to tell us what they meant.

A pin is "a statement someone has made, about anything." Every word starts as "an arrow in flight", and "needs to land somewhere to become a pin (noun)". So Pin is a mouse, a pin holds a note to the wall, and a pin is what someone said.

A semaphore, the last name on the S door, is how we unite terms around data, and data becomes information as they unite. As a verb, it routes pins toward places they can land, "by ripping seams open to find openings for users to provide nuance, correct our bias, and make the meaning personal".

The rule underneath: a name with several senses is one name for several truths, and must not be "resolved". The real hazard is two copies of one truth that can quietly drift apart, a cache and its file; those need a named tiebreaker. "Multiplicity of sense: keep. Duplication of fact: fence."

C4's own name is a seam. Its README calls it Collaborative Consent Continuity Controls; the public page on spread one said Continuous Creation, Command & Control. A note from 2026-08-19 says both were running then.

11

4 Doing

In C4, a word is a type when the system knows how to effect it: "give it real behavior, recognise it, use it." It does not need a row in the table. Corwin's words: "a virtual type simply means a word that we honor as it was a word we know how to effect as a type within our taxonomy even though it does not appear as a taxonomy".

His chosen example is LIST. The L line of m/t reads LAYOUT, LIBRARY, LINK, LICENSE. It has no LIST, and none is due. Yet the shop makes lists. A small file, l/l, pins each of eight brackets, ( ) [ ] { } < >, to its own hash, and that registry is what makes a list a list, with no table word anywhere in the loop. The file is on disk, and it is still marked CANDIDATE: the ruling settled the principle, not that one file.

This undoes a wrong question. "Is LIST in the table?" was checking the wrong ledger. The rule "decouples 'is this real?' from 'is this in the table?'" A type is real when something can do it.

C4-LANG lists six such words so far, each a word and a promise: TYPE (or kind), theme, skin, QUERY, word and hunk. One of them, word, holds the whole grammar together; it comes back on the keeping page.

5 Spaces

Two rules do most of the work in C4's grammar, and both show in a single line.

Space binds. A bracket right after a word, with no space, is an operator: in as(VIRTUAL), as is applied to VIRTUAL, one group. A bracket with a space before it is plain text, so as VIRTUAL is an operator and then a separate word. Because as( never occurs in ordinary English, it can end a run of unquoted prose exactly, with no quotes at all. Quoting and binding are "two delimiters for one job", so quotes never carry the weight alone; a language fenced only by quotes is one where "a stray quote changes meaning silently".

Case resolves. UPPERCASE words, such as VIRTUAL, TAXONOMY, HOST and TAG, are looked up in the taxonomy. Lowercase words are the language's own small words (as, from, toward, into) or the new name being made, taken just as written. One line can hold both: (TAG) at its head is looked up, and tag inside "(for C4: tag)" is literal.

That is why the short form is the normal one. Mint a name in lowercase and its other spellings come free; mint it in uppercase and you pay with a list. "The long one is the bill for choosing the harder case".

These rules were read off three of Corwin's own examples on 2026-08-10, after his nudge to "pick-up the clues about significance of space", and offered back for correction.

6 Keeping

C4's grammar has no error state. Its fallback type is word: anything that does not resolve "stands for itself". Every token either resolves or keeps its own text, so a line can always be rebuilt, byte for byte, from its pieces.

The same rule comes back at every height. A run nobody registered stays a word. A line nobody could parse stays, verbatim, as a comment. A check nobody could run is reported, never claimed as passed. The language "always has somewhere honest to put what it could not read".

"Could not check" is a third answer, beside yes and no, and it "must never share a channel with 'checked and passed'". The tools mean it: tv1cr answers a question it cannot answer with its own exit code, 2, and says so. When it overwrites a cell, it keeps the old value.

Rulings work the same way. In the shop's question bank, Q607 was DEFAULTED on 2026-10-19 and RULED on 2026-10-20; the ruling is a new row, and the default stays above it. To log, in C4, "is to ADD an atom, never to edit the past".

C4-LANG is careful here too. Keeping everything is right where dropping a human sentence would be the harm, and wrong where an unreadable input means the world is not as assumed. It is "a choice about which failure you prefer, not a strict improvement".

7 Proving

Every claim in C4 is meant to carry the tool that could break it: "a claim needs a tool that fails it", and its twin, "a test needs a mutant it kills, or it is only watching". A declaration can even "ship with its own falsifier, in the same sentence that makes the claim".

A passing test, alone, says only that a check passed today. Here it counts only if it was also shown to fail on a fault planted on purpose; tools take a --prove-red switch that plants the faults and expects red. Corwin's ghostwheel code starts that way: "Test scaffolding must come before everything".

The shop even keeps a liar. bin/sh1am says PASS for a file that is not there, and never admits it could not measure, on purpose. His reason: "we'll never prove any claims if we can't at least show a tool that DOESN'T do what we say we want ours to do." A gauge that passes sh1am is measuring nothing.

A check must be able to say no. When ot3he first ran on ten real acts, its verdict was NOT-MADE, and its record calls that "the strongest available evidence that it is measuring something".

Promises break early or late. His ladder for catching them runs testing, turn, build, install, run-time, bug reports. A test is the first rung.

This book keeps the rule: its checks plant a hard word on a picture page, and must turn red.

8 Twice

Spread two said C4 asks for two ways to say everything. Here is how to tell two ways from one way said twice: if either telling can be rebuilt from the other, the pair has collapsed; "only one register is actually present, and the second is decoration". 11 Zero

Is anyone home? Before a host can do anything else, it has to find out. In C4's shell library for the ghostwheel, ghostwheel.fn.sh, a run-level that is not set yet means "startup hasn't finished". The first level is 0, bootstrapping.

At level 0 the library runs only the tests "which can indicate startup issues". They ask plain questions. Are its four names filled in: C4_ID, C4_ROOT, C4_AS and C4_GW? Is the host it runs on really this host, by its long name and by its short one, and is "moo" not it? Every test the file declares today sits at level 0.

Last of all, the library defines a function "to confirm we exist!", which prints C4 GHOSTWHEEL and a version number. When you log in, the start-up file gw.rc loads the library and asks that function to speak. If the answer begins with C4, someone is home, and only then does it go on to bring the spokes up.

Notice what the tests do not do: they do not stop the machine. The file's rule is "Emit warnings about problems; no dying". A host that cannot answer every question at floor 0 still says what it found, and keeps going.

12 Floors

Above floor 0 the library names four floors, and their names spell C4: 1 containers, 2 components, 3 configurations, 4 connections. Then comes 5, running. C4.md lists "Containers Components Configurations Connections" as one of four four-C readings of the name.

A second ladder uses the same numbers. sg1pl, a small tool that fetches a value and checks it, climbs 0 read, 1 present, 2 fresh, 3 digest, 4 signed. Present: a real file you can read, not empty. Fresh: its time is not in the future. Digest: a sha256 file beside it matches. Signed: a gpg signature beside it verifies. Each level "does everything below it, then one more thing".

Corwin's ruling calls a move between the low levels a "tumbler change", and says an application climbing above them is still "running" to the host. An older note saw two ladders fighting over one digit; the ruling closed that.
